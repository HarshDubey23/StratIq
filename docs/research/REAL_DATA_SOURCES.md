# Anchor every synthetic Indian customer to data

Every agent parameter in a StrataIQ-style simulation can be grounded in open Indian or global data, but only by **layering an official rupee anchor under a fine-grained relative signal**. No open source gives rupee income at village, ward or grid level. The defensible approach is to take MoSPI's HCES 2023-24 consumption deciles (18 major states × rural/urban) as the absolute distribution. Agents are then placed within it by rank, using Meta's 2.4 km Relative Wealth Index and SHRUG's village-level SECC consumption estimates. WorldPop R2025A supplies 100 m age-sex counts, and Census 2011 houselisting tables give vehicle ownership down to village and ward. Self-hosted OSRM or Valhalla on OpenStreetMap supplies travel times, and HCES item shares supply category budgets. Customers choose a store with a Huff/multinomial logit model that has an outside option. Its outside-option constant is calibrated to a penetration rate and its price coefficient is backed out from a literature elasticity. The mathematics is peer-reviewed and well established. The weak links are behavioural parameters for Indian local services: there are **no India-calibrated distance-decay exponents** and **no India price elasticities for gyms, salons, dental care or tuition**, so those must enter as labelled assumptions with sensitivity analysis. Licensing is the other constraint. The two best spatial wealth layers are **non-commercial** (RWI is CC BY-NC 4.0; SHRUG is CC BY-NC-SA 4.0). That is fine for a hackathon. A commercial product would have to drop them or license them.

## Rupee anchors come from HCES, spatial ranks from RWI and SHRUG

The rich/poor ratio of a place needs two ingredients: an absolute distribution in rupees, and a way to tell which parts of a state sit where in it. For the first, HCES 2023-24 is the only current, official, openly published source. All-India average monthly per-capita consumption expenditure (MPCE) is **Rs 4,122 rural and Rs 6,996 urban**. Gini fell to **0.237 rural and 0.284 urban** from 0.266 and 0.314 in 2022-23 ([MoSPI press note](https://www.mospi.gov.in/sites/default/files/press_release/HCES_Press_Note_2023-24_27122024_rev.pdf)). The final report adds what a simulator actually needs. Table 3.1 gives 12 all-India fractile classes with upper limits and class means; the rural top 5% averages Rs 10,137 and the urban top 5% Rs 20,310. Tables 3.4R/3.4U give **P10–P90 deciles for 18 major states, rural and urban separately**. For example, the rural median runs from Rs 2,538 in Chhattisgarh to Rs 5,708 in Kerala, and the urban P90 from Rs 7,869 in Bihar to Rs 14,244 in Haryana ([HCES 2023-24 Final Report](https://mospi.gov.in/sites/default/files/publication_reports/Final_Report_HCES_2023-24L.pdf)). These tables are about 40 rows, a few kilobytes, and can be embedded directly in a backend. Unit-level microdata is listed on [microdata.gov.in catalog 237](https://microdata.gov.in/NADA/index.php/catalog/237), about 2.67 lakh households in 14,827 sample blocks. Its access and reuse terms could not be confirmed. Note that MPCE measures consumption, not income: label it "official aggregate (consumption proxy)" and use household spend ≈ MPCE × household size.

For spatial ranking, Meta's **Relative Wealth Index** covers every populated ~2.4 km Bing-tile cell in India. It predicts the DHS asset index from satellite and connectivity features, and India's DHS 2015-16 (598,733 households in 28,393 villages) was its largest training set. Accuracy against survey wealth is **R² 0.56–0.70**, depending on cross-validation design ([Chi et al., PNAS 2022 / arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.07761)). It is one ~19.7 MB India+Pakistan CSV with latitude, longitude, RWI and a per-cell error. HDX lists it under **CC BY-NC 4.0** ([HDX](https://data.humdata.org/dataset/relative-wealth-index)). The GEE community catalog says CC0 ([GEE catalog](https://gee-community-catalog.org/projects/rwi/)), but the publisher's host should be treated as binding. RWI is explicitly *relative within the country* and has no units, so it supplies ordering only.

At village and town level, **SHRUG v2.2** provides SECC-based per-capita consumption (2012 INR) and poverty rates for each "shrid", along with open polygons for about 600,000 villages and 8,000 towns. The consumption figures are small-area estimates: IHDS-II consumption is regressed on SECC asset and earnings dummies, then predicted for every SECC household ([SHRUG SECC consumption metadata](https://docs.devdatalab.org/SHRUG-Metadata/SECC%20consumption/secc-cons-metadata/)). The licence is **CC BY-NC-SA 4.0**, and commercial use needs a separate licence from Development Data Lab ([SHRUG](https://www.devdatalab.org/shrug)). The rawest within-village distribution comes from secc.gov.in. For every Gram Panchayat it publishes the share of households whose highest earner made <Rs 5,000, Rs 5–10k, or >Rs 10,000 a month. Bahavari GP in Shamli, for instance, is **67.45% / 23.91% / 8.65%** ([secc.gov.in](https://secc.gov.in/getAllCategoryIncomeSlabBlockLgdReport.htm/9/660/1436)). That is exactly a "rich/poor ratio", but it is from 2011, rural-only, published as HTML reports with no stated reuse licence, and it measures the top earner rather than the whole household.

The remaining candidates are secondary. VIIRS night lights (~500 m annual; EOG VNL under CC BY 4.0 for many products, or NASA Black Marble VNP46A4) track urban intensity but separate rich from poor poorly inside cities ([EOG](https://eogdata.mines.edu/products/vnl/); [LAADS DAAC](https://ladsweb.modaps.eosdis.nasa.gov/missions-and-measurements/science-domain/nighttime-lights/)). Even so, they are the only commercially safe fine-grained wealth covariate. NFHS-5 wealth quintiles are published at state level. Urban India has **45.5%** of people in the top national quintile versus **8.1%** in rural India ([Outlook Money](https://www.outlookmoney.com/news/chandigarh-delhi-punjab-have-highest-number-of-rich-people-half-of-rural-india-poorest-news-219370)). District shares would require DHS microdata, so they are best used to validate RWI. No open all-India district GDP series exists; Indiastat compilations are paywalled ([Indiastat Punjab](https://www.indiastatdistrictdemographics.com/punjab/all-districts/percapitaavailability/percapitaincomepci/data-year/2014-2023)). Census 2011 ward-level PCA has population, literacy and worker mix but **no income variable** ([Census NADA](https://censusindia.gov.in/nada/index.php/catalog/45261)).

## Rank matching turns relative wealth into rupees

The peer-reviewed bridge from an asset index to money is **percentile rank matching**, set out by Hruschka, Gerkey & Hadley and used by Chi et al. for their Absolute Wealth Estimate. Each unit's asset score is converted to a percentile rank, and consumption is set to x = F⁻¹(rank), where F is a parametric distribution fitted to official figures. Hruschka et al. fit F from the mean and Gini. For a lognormal, **σ = √2·Φ⁻¹((1+G)/2) and μ = ln x̄ − σ²/2**. Validated against World Bank poverty headcounts across 112 surveys, this reproduced them with **R² 0.80–0.84** ([Hruschka et al., Bull. WHO 2015](https://scielosp.org/pdf/bwho/2015.v93n7/483-490/en)). Chi et al. warn that their absolute estimates "should be treated with more caution than the RWI estimates" ([arXiv 2104.07761](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2104.07761)). No paper maps RWI specifically onto Indian HCES fractiles, so this pipeline is a principled composition rather than a validated Indian method.

India's published deciles make a richer fit possible than mean-plus-Gini. Given fractile shares u_k and class means m_k, the model-implied class mean is m̂_k(θ) = μ(θ)·[L(u_k;θ) − L(u_{k−1};θ)]/(u_k − u_{k−1}), where L is the Lorenz curve. You then minimise weighted squared log error. Hajargasht, Griffiths, Brice, Rao & Chotikapanich (2012, *JBES*) formalise this as GMM for grouped data. It gives standard errors and a J-test of fit, and they find **GB2 "fits the data well and outperforms" Singh-Maddala, Dagum and lognormal** ([IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/p/mlb/wpaper/1129.html)). The GB2 Lorenz curve is L(u) = I(I⁻¹(u;p,q); p+1/a, q−1/a), which requires q > 1/a. The R package GB2group implements it ([Jorda, Sarabia & Jäntti](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1808.09831)). Interpolating linearly between deciles is simpler, but it biases the Gini downward. A quick check shows the two-parameter lognormal is already adequate for a hackathon. Fitted only to the all-India rural mean and Gini (σ ≈ 0.43), it predicts a rural P10/P90 of about Rs 2,180/6,500 against the published Rs 2,260/6,414. The urban fit (σ ≈ 0.52) gives Rs 3,170/11,850 against Rs 3,313/11,546.

The concrete procedure for a catchment works in five steps.

1. **Rank the cells.** Compute the population-weighted empirical CDF of RWI across all cells in the state and sector, using mid-ranks to avoid 0 and 1.
2. **Map each cell to rupees.** Set each cell's central MPCE to F⁻¹(rank) using that state's HCES curve. Rescale so the population-weighted mean equals the official mean.
3. **Spread agents within each cell.** Draw agents from a rank window around the cell's rank, widened by RWI's reported error. Alternatively, draw from a within-cell lognormal whose σ_w makes between-cell plus within-cell variance reproduce the state Gini.
4. **Fuse SHRUG at village level.** SHRUG's SECC consumption can be rank-mapped the same way and blended with RWI. Rank mapping sidesteps the 2012-to-2024 CPI and methodology gap.
5. **Bucket into income classes.** Assign agents to classes such as poor < state P40, middle P40–P80, rich > P80 to produce the displayed rich/poor ratio.

The commonest error is ranking cells within a sub-region but drawing values from a national F. Ranks and distribution must come from the same geography ([methods per Chi et al. and Hruschka et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2104.07761)).

## Demographics, vehicles and travel time have strong open sources

Population and age-sex structure are the best-served parameters. **WorldPop R2025A** offers India rasters for 2015–2030 at 100 m, in a built-up-constrained version that suits placing agents on homes. The 2025 total-population GeoTIFF is 742 MB. The age-sex set is 62 files of about 740 MB each (0–1, then 5-year bands to 90+, by sex), roughly 46 GB in all, so download only the bands needed and sum pixels within catchments locally ([WorldPop R2025A directory](https://data.worldpop.org/GIS/AgeSex_structures/Global_2015_2030/R2025A/2025/IND/v1/100m/constrained/); [listing](https://hub.worldpop.org/geodata/listing?id=138)). The WorldPop REST stats API takes a GeoJSON polygon and returns an age-sex pyramid, but it covers only the older **2000–2020** global series ([WorldPop API](https://www.worldpop.org/sdi/advancedapi/)). The R2025A licence text was not confirmed; earlier WorldPop releases were CC BY 4.0. For urban/rural switching, **GHS-SMOD R2023A** gives a 1 km Degree-of-Urbanisation class (30 urban centre down to 11 very-low-density rural). **GHS-BUILT-S** gives 100 m built-up area with a non-residential band. Both may be reused with acknowledgement ([GEE GHS-SMOD](https://developers.google.com/earth-engine/datasets/catalog/JRC_GHSL_P2023A_GHS_SMOD_V2-0?hl=en); [GHS-BUILT-S](https://developers.google.com/earth-engine/datasets/catalog/JRC_GHSL_P2023A_GHS_BUILT_S)).

Household size and assets come from Census 2011. The **HL-14 houselisting tables** report the share of households owning a bicycle, two-wheeler, car, TV, phone and computer. They go down to village/town and ward, with total/rural/urban rows, as free XLSX files from the ORGI NADA catalog ([ORGI catalog 9324](https://censusindia.gov.in/nada/index.php/catalog/9324)). They are marked "All Rights Reserved" with no open licence. Household size can be computed as population ÷ households from the PCA. The tables are 15 years old, so their spatial pattern should be raked to recent totals. NFHS-5 has car ownership at about **8% nationally (14% urban, 4% rural)** and two-wheelers at about 54% ([ThePrint](https://theprint.in/india/only-8-indian-families-own-cars-nfhs-finds-over-50-still-use-bicycles-bikes-scooters/971413/)). HCES 2023-24 puts two-wheeler ownership above 60% with little rural-urban gap ([Data For India](https://www.dataforindia.com/vehicle-ownership-in-india/)). Raking needs a joint seed table, and India has no public PUMS-like seed. IPF from an independence seed returns the maximum-entropy joint, which is an assumption about how age, income and vehicle ownership correlate. Flag it in the provenance labels. Where household and person margins must both hold, IPU reweighting (Ye et al. 2009) is the standard tool. Validate with SRMSE and total absolute error ([Ye et al., TRB](https://scag.ca.gov/sites/main/files/file-attachments/populationsynthesizerpaper_trb.pdf); [Pritchard thesis](https://davidpritchard.org/masc_thesis/html/6_Evaluation.html)).

Road-network distance and time come from OpenStreetMap-based engines. Free hosted tiers are tight for a 100-home × 50-store matrix (5,000 cells). The **OSRM demo server** allows tables up to about 10,000 cells but only 1 request per second and non-commercial use ([OSRM wiki](https://github.com/Project-OSRM/osrm-backend/wiki/Demo-server); [R osrm docs](https://search.r-project.org/CRAN/refmans/osrm/html/osrmTable.html)). **openrouteservice's free plan** caps a matrix at 3,500 elements and 500 matrix requests a day ([ORS restrictions](https://openrouteservice.org/restrictions/); [ORS plans](https://openrouteservice.org/plans/)). GraphHopper's 500 free credits a day cannot afford the job ([GraphHopper FAQ](https://www.graphhopper.com/faq/what-is-one-credit/)). Valhalla's default matrix cap is 2,500 pairs ([Valhalla config](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/valhalla/valhalla/master/scripts/valhalla_build_config)). The production answer is to **self-host OSRM or Valhalla on a Geofabrik India extract**, with no licence fee. No free engine ships a two-wheeler profile, which matters because two-wheelers are India's dominant mode. Mode choice and distance tolerance come from the new MoSPI **National Household Travel Survey 2025-26** (492,023 households). It reports that **two-wheelers carry 42.6% of work trips (52.3% urban)**, walking 28.3%, an average commute of 25 minutes, and 83.4% arriving within 30 minutes ([Adda247 summary](https://currentaffairs.adda247.com/indias-first-national-household-travel-survey-2025-26-key-findings/); [Construction World](https://www.constructionworld.in/amp/policy-updates-and-economic-news/two-wheelers-lead-work-commutes-as-buses-rail-dominate-overnight-travel/98517)). These figures come from secondary summaries; the primary factsheet was blocked. Census 2011 table B-28 cross-tabulates commute distance band by mode down to district and city ([ORGI catalog 13954](https://censusindia.gov.in/nada/index.php/catalog/13954)). Competitor locations come from **Overture Places** (CDLA Permissive 2.0 / Apache 2.0, monthly GeoParquet, no account needed) and **Foursquare OS Places** (Apache 2.0, free token). Both allow commercial use, and both have known duplicate and junk rates ([Overture docs](https://docs.overturemaps.org/guides/places/); [Foursquare docs](https://docs.foursquare.com/data-products/docs/access-fsq-os-places)).

## Behavioural parameters are the weakest link

Category budgets are well grounded. HCES 2023-24 Table 3.5 gives all-India rural/urban shares of MPCE:

| Category | Rural share of MPCE | Urban share of MPCE |
|---|---|---|
| Beverages, refreshments and processed food | 9.84% | 11.09% |
| Education | 3.24% | 5.97% |
| Medical | 6.83% | 5.85% |
| Conveyance | 7.59% | 8.46% |
| Consumer services excluding conveyance (includes salons) | 5.25% | 5.72% |
| Durable goods | 6.48% | 6.87% |

Sub-item tables add toilet articles (about 5.2%) and entertainment (1.02% rural, 1.77% urban), which were machine-extracted and need a spot-check ([HCES 2023-24 Final Report](https://mospi.gov.in/sites/default/files/publication_reports/Final_Report_HCES_2023-24L.pdf)). The processed-food line is not "eating out". It also includes packaged food for the home, so cafés and restaurants should use the cooked-meals sub-item. Shares are not published by fractile, so the income gradient needs an Engel curve. The Working-Leser form, w_c = α_c + β_c·ln x, gives an expenditure elasticity η_c = 1 + β_c/w_c ([FAO](https://www.fao.org/3/y4475e/y4475e07.htm)). Indian estimates exist for food and health. QUAIDS on Karnataka NSS data gives fruit at about 1.3 and meat at 1.6–2.0 ([Umanath et al., IJAE 2015](https://isaeindia.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/11/02-Article-Umanath.pdf)). Health spending is a necessity, with an income elasticity of about **0.25–0.5** ([Dubey, NIPFP WP 324](https://www.nipfp.org.in/media/documents/WP_324_2020.pdf)). For services such as education, entertainment and personal care, budget shares are documented as rising with expenditure (elasticity > 1), but without clean coefficients ([Nayyar 2009](https://catalog.ihsn.org/citations/9366)).

Price elasticity is thinner. The 160-study meta-analysis by Andreyeva, Long & Brownell puts food away from home among the most responsive food groups, at **|η| ≈ 0.7–0.8**. The studies are mostly from the US ([AJPH 2010](https://fds.duke.edu/db/aas/pn/faculty/kelly.brownell/publications/271328)). Indian food own-price elasticities include milk at −0.86 (urban) and fruit at −1.1 ([Umanath et al.](https://isaeindia.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/11/02-Article-Umanath.pdf)). No India or LMIC own-price elasticity was found for gyms, salons, dental care or tuition. Finer disaggregation also raises elasticities, so outlet-level price sensitivity far exceeds category-level sensitivity ([Cleghorn et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1909.13179)). The −0.8 figure should therefore constrain total category demand, not switching between neighbouring shops.

Distance decay has no Indian calibration at all. Esri's Huff default is a power exponent of **1.5, "usually 1.5–2"**, with grocery decaying faster than furniture ([Esri](https://pro.arcgis.com/en/pro-app/tool-reference/business-analyst/understanding-huff-model.htm)). Esri's calibrated example gives about −2.7 ([Esri white paper](https://www.esri.com/content/dam/esrisites/en-us/arcgis/products/business-analyst/assets/calibrating-huff-model.pdf)). SafeGraph-calibrated US supermarkets mostly fall at **0.4–1.0**, lower in denser cities ([Liang et al., Trans. GIS 2020](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2003.10857)). A reasonable prior is about 2.0–2.7 for walk-in convenience trades, 1.5–2.0 for cafés and salons, and 1.0–1.5 for planned repeat services such as gyms, dentists and coaching. These are interpolations, not measurements.

Participation rates are the most patchy. Anchors exist for most categories:

| Category | Participation anchor | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Gyms | **0.8%** of the population hold memberships (12.3 m in 2024; most in value gyms; 56% of revenue in the top 10 cities) | [Deloitte/HFA 2025](https://www.deloitte.com/in/en/about/press-room/indias-fitness-market-to-double-by-2030-per-a-deloitte-and-hfa-report.html) |
| Dental care | About **18–24%** of adults visited a dentist in the past year (I² ≈ 99%) | [Talukdar et al. 2022](https://hpp.tbzmed.ac.ir/FullHtml/hpp-40726) |
| Private coaching | **27.0%** of students (25.5% rural, 30.7% urban, 37.8% at secondary); urban spend Rs 13,026 a year | [CMS: Education 2025 analysis](https://educationforallinindia.com/is-school-education-in-india-truly-free-nsso-cms-education-2025/) |
| Eating out | About **3.7 dine-outs and 4.2 delivery orders a month** among urban consumers (an industry sample, not population-representative) | [NRAI IFSR via 5paisa](https://sandbox.5paisa.com/news/indians-prefer-ordering-in-over-dining-out) |
| Salons | No citable figure; derive from the consumer-services share ÷ ticket price | n/a |

No published India willingness-to-pay figures exist for these services.

## One utility function assembles every parameter

Store choice combines all of the above in one framework. **Huff's gravity model is exactly a multinomial logit**: P_ij = A_j^γ d_ij^(−λ) / Σ_k A_k^γ d_ik^(−λ) is MNL with V_ij = γ ln A_j − λ ln d_ij ([MCI package manual](https://cran.r-universe.dev/MCI/doc/manual.html); [Train Ch. 3](http://eml.berkeley.edu/books/choice2nd/Ch03_p34-75.pdf)). Each agent n gets the utility

V_nj = c + γ ln A_j − λ_c ln t_nj − α_c ln(p_j / R_n) + ε,

with an outside option V_n0 = 0. The terms map to the data as follows:

| Term | Meaning | Source |
|---|---|---|
| t_nj | Travel time by the agent's mode | Routing engine; mode drawn from vehicle ownership and SMOD class |
| R_n | Reference budget, R_n = x_n · w_c(x_n) / f_c | x_n: consumption from rank matching; w_c: Engel-adjusted HCES share; f_c: purchase frequency |
| f_c | Purchase frequency per period | Assumption |
| α_c | Price coefficient | From a literature elasticity. In MNL, η_jj = −α p_j(1 − s_j), so α = −η/(p_j(1 − s_j)) with s_j the unconditional share. With ln p, η = −α(1 − s_j) ([Structural IO notes](https://mike-data-analysis.share.connect.posit.cloud/sec-structural-io.html)) |
| c | Common constant | Solved by bisection so modelled penetration (1/N)Σ_n(1 − P_n0) equals the observed rate, e.g. 0.8% for gyms or ~0.2 a year for dentists. Penetration rises monotonically in c |

If store-level totals are known, the BLP/Berry contraction δ_j ← δ_j + ln S_j − ln Ŝ_j matches every store's share, and penetration with it. This is the same fixed point as Güssefeldt's Huff attractiveness calibration. One identification trap: when every A_j is free, any λ fits the totals exactly. λ is identified only if attractiveness is tied to observables, such as floor area or rating, or if visit data exist ([MCI manual](https://cran.r-universe.dev/MCI/doc/manual.html)).

Two refinements address MNL's weaknesses. First, IIA means a new entrant draws proportionally from every incumbent. A nested logit (buy in category vs. not) relaxes this, though its σ cannot be identified from aggregate shares and must be swept as an assumption ([Berry 1994 via Structural IO notes](https://mike-data-analysis.share.connect.posit.cloud/sec-structural-io.html)). Second, awareness can gate each agent's choice set through an agent-level Bass process. Each period, an unaware agent becomes aware with probability 1 − exp[−(p + q·a_n)Δt], where a_n is the aware share of its neighbours. The meta-analytic averages are **p ≈ 0.03 and q ≈ 0.38 per year**, but they come from consumer durables, not local shops ([Bass diffusion summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bass_diffusion_model)).

Because the behavioural inputs are uncertain, scenario outputs should be Monte Carlo distributions, not point forecasts. The inputs to vary are λ, η, σ, the Bass p and q, the GB2 parameters and the within-cell spread. Each draw must rerun the full calibration loop. Sobol total-effect indices (Jansen/Saltelli estimators, N(k+2) runs via SALib) then show which assumptions drive revenue ([R sensitivity docs](https://rdrr.io/cran/sensitivity/man/soboljansen.html)).

## Conclusion

The data gap in Indian market simulation lies in behaviour, not geography. Population, wealth rank, assets, roads and competitors can all be resolved to 100 m–2.4 km from open data. How far an Indian customer will ride a two-wheeler for a haircut, or how much a gym price cut shifts membership, has no Indian evidence at all. The scientifically defensible move is to make that asymmetry visible. Pin the observable layers hard to official totals through rank matching and raking. Calibrate the outside option to penetration so aggregate demand is right by construction. Treat λ, η and σ as distributions whose Sobol indices tell users which assumption their decision actually hinges on. The first local footfall dataset, whether from mobility panels or a pilot business's own sales, would be worth more than any additional open layer, because it is the only thing that can identify λ.

Two practical implications follow. First, the commercial path forks early. A product built on HCES, WorldPop, GHSL, night lights, OSM and Overture is commercially clean. Adding RWI and SHRUG buys much sharper village-level wealth contrast but forces a non-commercial licence, share-alike on derived files, or a paid DDL licence. Second, no published work validates RWI-to-HCES rank mapping against Indian district consumption. Running that check is a concrete contribution: NFHS-5 district wealth quintiles or HCES unit-level district estimates are the obvious benchmarks, and the result would turn the "derived estimate" label into a measured error bar.
